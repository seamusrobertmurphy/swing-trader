**Rationale**: Both sides made sharp points, but when I strip away the rhetoric, the bear wins on evidence quality and the bull wins mostly on narrative and cherry-picked analogies. Let me walk through why.

The bull's entire macro case rests on "rates will eventually fall" and "sentiment at 3/10 always precedes a rally." The bear correctly points out that markets price forward expectations, not eventual outcomes — the Fed holding through 2026 with cuts not expected until Q1 2027 is already known and largely priced in. The bull's historical analogies (Nov 2022, March 2020, May 2023) all had identifiable, immediate catalysts (Fed pivot signals, SVB crisis, COVID stimulus) forcing a regime shift. The bear's rebuttal that "no such catalyst currently exists" is the more falsifiable, evidence-grounded claim, and the bull never actually names a present-tense trigger — only prospective, uncertain ones 2-5+ months out (or 2028 halving, which is irrelevantly distant).

On the miner story, the bull's "dual revenue stream = bullish optionality" argument is plausible in isolation, but the bear's read is more consistent with the hard numbers: miners are up 90% while BTC is down 44%, meaning the market itself is pricing this as a rotation of capital and attention away from pure Bitcoin exposure. The bear's point about reduced marginal capex into hash rate and increased latent sell pressure (miners needing BTC-denominated liquidity to fund AI capex) is a legitimate structural risk the bull hand-waves as "optionality."

On technicals, the bull's "compression precedes breakout, and it'll be up because sentiment is bearish" is an assertion, not a derivation. The bear's rebuttal — MACD histogram still accelerating negative, RSI showing bearish divergence (price down only 5% but RSI down 18 points), and volume collapsing rather than spiking on down days — is a more rigorous read of the same data. Compression precedes a move, but nothing in the data set actually signals which direction; the bear's case that the path of least resistance is down given the trend context is more defensible.

The sentiment argument is the closest call. Capitulation-level sentiment is a legitimate contrarian signal, but the bear's key insight is correct: capitulation without any footprint of institutional buying (no ETF inflow acceleration, no volume spike on weakness) is just extended selling, not proof of accumulation. The bull asserts "quiet institutional accumulation" without any of the report's evidence actually showing net accumulation — that's an inference, not a data point.

Finally, on the expected-value math, the bull's 70%/40% upside vs 30%/25% downside numbers are simply invented with no grounding in the report, while the bear's scenario-weighted estimate (using more conservative and defensible base rates) comes out modestly negative. I don't fully buy the bear's precise numbers either, but the process discipline favors the bear.

Net: the bear's case is more grounded in what the data actually shows right now (deteriorating momentum, no active catalyst, capital quietly leaving the mining/BTC nexus for AI, valuation headwind from real rates), while the bull's case requires several forward-looking catalysts to land within a fairly tight window. This doesn't rise to full Sell/short conviction, because the bull's structural long-term points (scarcity, institutional ETF adoption, eventual Fed normalization) aren't wrong, just premature — and a full short carries real tail risk if a surprise catalyst (Trump policy, geopolitical shock) hits. That asymmetry argues for trimming/underweighting rather than aggressively shorting or holding at current sizing.
