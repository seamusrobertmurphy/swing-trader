Working Notes
Here is the scoping metric as a concrete, codeable spec. No actual code, but precise enough that the model at home can build it directly. Think of it as one screening function that takes a coin and returns a pass or fail with numbers attached.
The inputs per coin, all pullable from CCXT OHLCV. You fetch daily candles, say the last 90 to 180 days, and from them compute four numbers.
One, liquidity. 24-hour quote volume in USDT, straight from the ticker. This is a hard gate, computed first, because if it fails nothing else matters. Set a floor, for a proving-stage account something modest but real, in the order of tens of millions in daily USDT volume keeps you in genuinely liquid names. Below the floor, reject immediately and do not compute anything else.
Two, volatility, the ATR band. Compute 14-day ATR, then divide by current price to get ATR as a percent. This is your headline volatility number. Test it against two thresholds: a floor and a ceiling. The floor is tied to your edge requirement, ATR percent must exceed roughly your net edge floor so a normal multi-day move can reach the target. The ceiling rejects the detonating names. Both thresholds are parameters passed into the function, not hard-coded, so walk-forward can tune them later.
Three, spread. Pull the current best bid and ask from the order book, compute the percentage gap between them. This is the per-trade tax. Reject anything with a wide spread because it quietly kills the edge. A tight threshold here, well under your fee, is a hard gate like liquidity.
Four, history sufficiency. Count how many candles actually came back. If a coin has too few days of history it cannot have lived through multiple regimes and its indicators are unreliable, so reject coins below a minimum candle count. This is the same skip-too-new logic the day-metrics document already uses.
The output. For each coin the function returns the four raw numbers plus a single pass or fail, where pass means it cleared all four gates. You run it across the whole CCXT universe or a sensible slice of it, collect the results in a table, and what survives is your candidate universe. That table is the deliverable: coin, volume, ATR percent, spread percent, candle count, pass or fail. You read it, you eyeball the survivors, you pick your fifteen to twenty-five to scan from.
Two design notes that matter more than the thresholds.
The thresholds live in one config block, not scattered through the code. Every number, the volume floor, the ATR floor and ceiling, the spread limit, the minimum history, sits in one place, passed in as parameters. This is the program.md principle applied to screening: the human owns these numbers, they are visible and tunable in one spot, and later they are the things walk-forward optimises and that stay outside the model's reach.
The screen runs as a scheduled job, not once. Liquidity and volatility drift, so a coin that passes today may fail in a month. Run the screen on a cadence, weekly fits your review rhythm, and let the candidate universe refresh itself. The output table dated and saved each run also becomes part of your experiment log, the Karpathy artefact you wake up to.
One honest caution as a closing note. CCXT giving you 205 exchanges is power but also a trap. Do not scan all of them. Most of those exchanges carry thin, duplicated, or unreliable listings, and breadth there is noise, not opportunity. Screen within the venue you actually execute on, Binance, so that a coin passing the screen is a coin you can really trade. The wide library is for flexibility in which venue, not for casting a net across all of them at once.
That is the metric: a four-gate screen, liquidity and spread as hard gates, ATR as a tunable band, history as a sufficiency check, thresholds in one human-owned config, run weekly, scoped to your execution venue, output as a dated table of survivors. It is small enough to build quickly and it is the concrete front door to everything else in the manifesto. Good place to hand it to the model at home.

Scoping: Holding Period
Good, this is the right thing to stop on, because the hold period is upstream of almost everything else we have decided and you are right that you picked it on gut. Let me give you the structure to choose it properly rather than another guess.
The hold period is really one question: how long do you give a trade to work before you are either out with profit or out with a loss. Everything else bends to that answer. It sets which ATR band makes sense, how often you trade, how much fees bite, how much screen attention the thing needs, and which signals even mean anything. So it is worth getting deliberately rather than by feel.
Here is the spectrum, honestly, from fast to slow.
Scalping, seconds to minutes. You take tiny moves many times a day. We already ruled this out and the reason is final: at 0.2 percent round-trip fees, the moves are too small to clear costs, and it demands constant attention. Dead for you.
Day trading, minutes to hours, closed out same day. Faster than swing, needs you watching screens through the session, and on the Alpaca equity side it is legally blocked by the pattern-day-trader rule at your account size. Possible on crypto but it fights everything we built: it is attention-heavy and fee-sensitive in exactly the ways your patience instinct was trying to avoid.
Swing trading, days to a couple of weeks. You hold through the noise and aim for a larger, cleaner move. Fees become trivial because a 5 percent swing does not care about 0.2 percent. It needs checking once or twice a day, not constant watching. This is where your stated patience, your two-venue split, and your "I'm happy to wait 48 hours for a clear setup" all actually live. Your repo is named trader-swing. Everything has been quietly pointing here.
Position trading, weeks to months. Slower still, closer to investing, driven more by thesis than by the technical signals your model is built from. Your MACD-and-friends stack is not really a months-long tool, and this timescale underuses the machinery you have already built.
So the honest read: your gut answer of one-to-three days was not naive, it landed inside the swing band, which is the band every other decision today already assumed. The question is whether you want the tighter end of swing, one to three days, or the looser end, up to a week or two.
Now the part you actually asked, the relationship between hold period and ATR, because you saw it correctly. They are locked together. If you hold one to three days, the coin must move enough in one to three days to reach your profit target, so it needs a higher daily ATR. If you give a trade up to two weeks, a calmer coin can still get there because it has more days to accumulate the move, so you can accept a lower daily ATR. Shorter holds demand more daily volatility; longer holds tolerate less. That is the whole coupling. Your worry, that a coin not moving enough will miss a one-to-three-day target, is exactly right, and the lever that fixes it is either pick livelier coins or lengthen the hold. Those are the same dial seen from two ends.
Which way to lean, given your real situation. Three things push you toward the longer end of swing, a week or two rather than one to three days. First, attention: you said you want to read the market broadly and not obsess over trades, and longer holds need less watching. Second, the account is a five-hundred-dollar proving rig, so trade frequency should be low to keep fees from eating it, and longer holds mean fewer trades. Third, longer holds let you trade calmer, deeper, more liquid coins, which is safer while you are still validating that the system works at all. The one thing pulling shorter is that shorter holds give you more trades, hence more data to validate the model faster, which at a proving stage has real value.
So my honest suggestion, and it is a suggestion not an answer: do not fix a single number. Make the hold period a parameter with a range, say one to ten days, and let walk-forward tell you where in that range the edge actually lives, the same way it sets the ATR band and the fee floor. You chose one-to-three on gut; the data should choose the real number. What you fix now is the band, swing not scalp and not position, and that band is already baked into everything we built. Where inside it you settle is a thing to discover, not declare.
For this afternoon, that means do not screen coins against a rigid one-to-three-day ATR cutoff. Screen for the swing band generally: coins liquid enough to trust and lively enough to move a few percent over several days. That keeps your options open while the model later finds the exact hold length that pays.

Scoping: ATR Metrics
Quick correction on the definition first, because it changes how you use it this afternoon. ATR is not a percentage of the coin's value over its lifespan. It is the coin's typical movement over one period, a single day for your purposes, averaged across the last fourteen days. So daily ATR answers how much does this coin usually move in a day, right now, not across its whole history. Read as a percent of current price, a coin with 3 percent daily ATR typically swings about 3 percent in a day. That is the number you eyeball this afternoon: roughly how much ground does a normal day cover, because that is what has to carry price to your profit target inside one to three days.

You are right to want it front and centre, and it belongs in two distinct places that are easy to conflate.
First, as a selection filter, the gate that decides which coins enter the universe at all. This is the afternoon job: ATR band as the screen that admits or rejects a coin before the model ever sees it. Second, as a live guardrail, the band held outside the model's reach that keeps it from trading a coin that has drifted out of the tradable range. Same metric, two jobs, both currently absent.
Let me write it into the still-open section of the manifesto now so the model at home has it as a concrete build item rather than a principle we discussed and lost.Done. The ATR band is now written into the manifesto as its own section, flagged explicitly as not yet built and missing from the notebook, so whoever picks it up at home cannot mistake it for something already coded.
One thing on your worry that ATR may be an obstruction in crypto. It is not, and crypto is where it earns its keep most. ATR was designed for any freely traded market and crypto's wide, varied volatility is exactly the spread it sorts well: it cleanly separates a steady major moving a few percent a day from a meme coin swinging twenty, which is precisely the distinction you need this afternoon. The only crypto wrinkle is that crypto trades twenty-four seven, so there are no overnight gaps between sessions the way equities have, which actually makes daily ATR cleaner in crypto, not messier. So use it with confidence as your scoping tool.
For this afternoon, the practical move is simple. On each coin you are eyeing, look at roughly how much it moves on a normal day as a percent of price. Anything that barely moves cannot reach your target in one to three days and is out. Anything that swings wildly enough that you cannot imagine where a stop would even sit is out the other side. What is left, the coins that move a few percent a day reliably without detonating, is your tradable band. You do not need to compute ATR by hand to start; the eyeball version gets you a candidate list, and the model fits the exact cutoffs later from the data.


Scoping: Volatility
The volatility filter uses ATR, average true range, read as a percentage of the coin's price so different coins are comparable. It is a band with two edges: a floor, because a coin must move enough in your one-to-three-day window to actually reach your profit target after fees, and a ceiling, because past a certain point the coin gaps unpredictably through your stop and take-profit and stops being something a swing model can position around. The floor sits above your net edge requirement, so above roughly two to three percent daily ATR if your edge floor is two percent; the ceiling sits below where the coin starts detonating, which on crypto is often the very high-ATR meme and micro names. You do not pick those cutoffs from a feeling, you fit them with walk-forward and let the out-of-sample results pick the band that actually produced edge after fees. And like the fee floor, the band must sit outside the model's reach, or a model rewarded for activity would simply widen its own ceiling to justify more trades.
Not quite a too-volatile ceiling. What I said was the opposite end first: volatility as a floor, a minimum amount of movement a coin must have before it is worth trading at all. But you have intuited the second half correctly, there is also a ceiling, and a good volatility filter is a band with both. Let me lay out both edges and the actual metric.
The metric is ATR, average true range. It measures how much a coin typically moves in a given period, capturing the full range including gaps, not just close-to-close. Expressed as a percentage of price it is comparable across coins: a 200-dollar coin and a 0.50-dollar coin can be compared directly if you read ATR as a percent of price rather than in dollars. That percentage is your volatility number. The standard is ATR over a 14-period window, on whatever timeframe matches your hold; for a one-to-three-day swing you would look at daily ATR.
Now the two edges.
The floor, which is what I originally meant. A coin must move enough, per day, to clear your minimum-edge floor inside your holding window. If your edge floor is around two percent net and you hold one to three days, a coin whose daily ATR is half a percent simply cannot get you there; the move you need does not exist in that coin on that timescale. So the rule is roughly: daily ATR percent must be comfortably larger than your net edge floor, so that a normal day's range can actually carry price to your take-profit. This is where your volatility instinct becomes a real filter. You like big moves; this is the number that says how big is big enough to bother.
The ceiling, which is what you just intuited. Above some level, volatility stops being opportunity and becomes unforecastable chaos. The day-metrics document already flagged this: the more violent the asset the riskier its forecast. Past a certain ATR the coin gaps through your stop and your take-profit unpredictably, slippage widens, and the indicators that your model relies on lose meaning because price is being thrown around by single events rather than tradable structure. So there is an upper ATR band beyond which you decline, not because you fear movement but because the movement is no longer something a one-to-three-day model can position around.
The honest part you need to hear: I cannot hand you exact percentage cutoffs that are correct in the abstract, and you should distrust anyone who does. The right floor and ceiling depend on your edge floor, your hold length, your stop width, and the fee. They are parameters you fit, not constants I recite. What I can give you is the shape. The floor sits above your net edge requirement, so above roughly two to three percent daily ATR if your edge floor is two percent, because the day's range has to exceed the gain you are reaching for. The ceiling sits wherever the coin starts gapping through your risk levels, which on crypto is often the very high ATR names, the new and thin and meme coins, frequently ten percent-plus daily ranges. The tradable band for a patient swing strategy tends to live in the middle, liquid majors and established alts that move a few percent a day reliably without detonating. But those numbers are starting points to calibrate against your own backtest, not law.
How to set them properly rather than guess. This is a thing walk-forward decides for you. You make the ATR floor and ceiling tunable parameters, run the validation across regimes, and let the out-of-sample results tell you which band actually produced tradable edge after fees. The data picks the band; you do not pick it from a feeling. And critically, the band is one of the parameters that must sit outside the model's self-evaluation, same as the fee floor, because a model that could widen its own volatility ceiling to access more trades would do exactly that to book more activity.
So the volatility filter is a band, not a single number. ATR percent of price as the metric, a floor set above your edge requirement so a coin moves enough to be worth it, a ceiling set below where the coin gaps through your risk controls, both fit by walk-forward rather than asserted, both held outside the model's reach. It interacts directly with the edge floor and the take-profit we already defined: floor below take-profit, ATR floor above the move you need to reach take-profit. They are one coupled system.
That coupling is also why my account-size question keeps mattering, and I will stop asking after this: position size sets how much slippage and how thin a fill you can tolerate, which shifts where the practical ATR ceiling sits. A small account can trade higher-ATR coins because small orders barely move the book; a larger account hits depth limits and has to stay in the calmer, deeper names. So the account size quietly sets one edge of this band. Ballpark in USDT whenever you want to nail it down.
Scoping: Tradability & Regime Diversity
Let me take the definitions first, then the whales, then the emotive-coin question, because they connect.
Tradability means: can you actually get in and out at the price you see, in the size you want, without your own order moving the market against you. Three things determine it. Liquidity, meaning sustained real volume and a deep order book, so there are buyers when you sell and sellers when you buy. Spread, meaning the gap between bid and ask, because a wide spread is a tax you pay on every round trip and it quietly kills scalping margins. And slippage, meaning how far the price moves between your decision and your fill. A coin can look like it printed a clean ten percent move on the chart, but if the book is thin you could never have captured it at size. Tradability is the filter that separates what looks profitable in a backtest from what is actually profitable when real money hits the book. For scalping especially, where your edge per trade is small, tradability is not one factor among many, it is the gate. A thin coin with a great-looking pattern is a trap.
Regime diversity means: does your training data contain all the market states the model will face in the wild. A regime is a sustained mode of behaviour. Trending up, trending down, ranging sideways, low volatility grind, high volatility chaos. A model trained only on a bull stretch learns that dips get bought and breaks out, and then it gets destroyed the first time the market ranges or sells off, because it has never seen that state and confidently does the wrong thing. Regime diversity is why a single day's top-volume snapshot is dangerous: it is one regime, one slice of one news cycle. You want a training window long enough and a coin set varied enough that the model has seen bull, bear, chop, calm, and panic, ideally several of each. That is what lets it generalise rather than memorise a mood.
So when I said select by tradability and regime diversity, I meant: pick coins you can actually execute on cleanly, over a history that contains every market state you expect to trade through. Those two criteria, not raw count, define the set.
Now whales. Your instinct is sharper than the random-noise framing I gave earlier, and you are right to push on it. There are two different things flying under one word. One is structural whale behaviour: large holders accumulating or distributing, which leaves real, learnable footprints in order flow, volume spikes, and price action. That is signal. A model can in principle learn to detect accumulation or a distribution dump. The other is single-actor manipulation on a tiny illiquid coin: one wallet pumping a microcap, which is not learnable structure, it is one person's whim, and it looks like signal right up until it ruins you. The distinction is liquidity again. On a deep, liquid coin, whale activity is one large participant among many and leaves statistical traces worth modelling. On a thin coin, the whale is the market and there is nothing to generalise. So yes, build a whale-detection submodule, but build it on liquid coins where the footprint is a pattern, not a single hand on the wheel. Test it as its own module against the rest of your signals, which is exactly the right way to find out if it has edge.
The media-and-public-figure point is real and exploitable, but be clear-eyed about what kind of strategy it is. That is event-driven trading, reacting to a catalyst, and it lives on a different timescale and infrastructure than chart-pattern scalping. To trade it you need a fast news or social feed and latency, because by the time a move is visible on the chart the edge is mostly gone. It is a legitimate submodule, but it is a sensing problem more than a price-pattern problem, so flag it as a separate track, not something the price-trained model will pick up on its own.
Now the emotive coins, and here I will be direct because it matters to your money. Your interest in what a coin is trying to do, Uniswap, the Ethereum spinoffs, Solana, the DeFi and decentralisation thesis, is genuinely valuable, but it is the wrong input for this model. What a coin aspires to be is a thesis about its long-term value. Day trading and scalping operate on minutes and hours, where narrative does not move the next candle; order flow and microstructure do. The passion and the politics are real and they may be why certain coins have sustained community and volume, which is good for liquidity, but the model should care about whether a coin is liquid and whether it exhibits learnable structure, not whether its mission excites you. The risk of letting the emotive read drive coin selection is that you train on coins you find interesting rather than coins you can profitably trade, and those are not the same set. Let conviction inform your watchlist if you like, but make tradability the hard filter on top of it.
Where this leaves the unorthodox-versus-conventional choice you flagged at the end: the conventional approach is not the boring opposite of your instincts. It is the discipline that lets your instincts pay off. The whale submodule and the event-driven track are the genuinely unorthodox, edge-seeking pieces, and they are best built as separate, individually tested modules on top of a model trained conventionally on a clean, liquid, regime-diverse base. That way each unorthodox idea gets proven or killed on its own evidence rather than quietly contaminating the core.

Scoping: Sample Size Trade-Offs
This is a real fork, and it has a known shape in machine learning, so let me give you the substance rather than just filing it.
The tension you are describing is breadth of training universe versus depth of signal. Both extremes fail, for different reasons.
Train on hundreds of coins because Binance lists them and you inherit two problems. First, most small or new coins are noise, not signal. Their price action is driven by illiquidity, single whales, and pump-and-dump mechanics, not by the kind of repeatable microstructure a model can learn from. Feeding that in teaches the model to fit randomness, which is overfitting dressed up as coverage. Second, you cannot actually trade most of that universe well. Thin order books mean slippage eats your edge, and a backtest on illiquid coins lies to you because it assumes fills you would never get. So the wide purview that helps a human eyeball opportunities is a liability when you are training a model to act.
Train on ten high-volume coins picked by one day's volume and you have the opposite failure. The sample is arbitrary, it has survivorship and recency bias baked in, and ten is too few to learn anything that generalises. One day's top volume is a snapshot of that day's news cycle, not a stable population.
The resolution is not a point on the line between ten and five hundred. It is to separate two things you are currently treating as one: the universe you train on and the universe you trade on.
Train on a curated, liquid, reasonably stable set, large enough to capture varied regimes but filtered hard for tradability. Something like the top thirty to fifty coins by sustained volume and order-book depth over a trailing window, not a single day. You want enough names to see bull, bear, and chop across different coins, and enough history per name that the model learns structure rather than a particular month. The model learns general microstructure behaviour from this clean set.
Then, at inference, you scan wide. The scanning purview that you value from Binance lives at the trading stage, where you point the trained model at a broader watchlist and let it score opportunities, with liquidity filters as a hard gate before any trade. The wide net catches candidates; the model trained on clean data judges them; the liquidity gate stops you acting on coins you cannot actually exit.
So the answer to "how big should the training set be" is: bigger than ten, smaller than everything, and selected by tradability and regime diversity rather than by raw count or one day's volume. The selection criteria matter far more than the number. The notes should record the criteria, not a target N.
One thing worth flagging for the model at home. Alpaca and Binance are different universes with different available coins, fee structures, and data granularity, and Alpaca's crypto offering is much narrower than Binance. If the bot trades on Alpaca, training on Binance-only coins that Alpaca does not list is wasted. The training universe has to intersect with what you can actually execute on. Worth pinning down which venue is the execution target before finalising the coin list, because that constraint may decide the question for you.

Fences &  Guardrails
That is a good instinct and it is worth writing down, with one sharpening so it does the work you actually want.
What you are describing is a minimum-edge threshold, a margin floor set deliberately above the breakeven fee line, so the model is forbidden from taking trades whose expected gain only just clears the fee. Not a brake on trading, a brake on marginal trading. The crypto round trip costs roughly 0.2 percent, so you set the floor much higher, say a trade must project at least one and a half or two percent of expected move before it is allowed to fire. Everything between the fee waterline and that floor is the zone where fees dominate and where a performance-driven model is tempted to churn. You are fencing that zone off.
But sharpen it on two points or it will not behave the way you want.
First, decide whether it is an alarm or a fence, because you said both and they are different. An alarm tells you a trade is near the waterline and lets it through. A fence refuses the trade. For the behaviour you are worried about, the model hiding thin trades in volume, an alarm is not enough, because the model can ring the bell and trade anyway and the losses still accumulate. You want the floor to be a hard fence the model cannot cross, set in your code, not a flag the model can note and override. Keep the alarm too if you like, as a softer warning band just above the fence, but the floor itself has to refuse.
Second, the floor has to be measured against expected net move, after fees and after slippage, not gross. A trade that projects one percent gross on Binance is really about 0.8 percent once the round trip is paid, less again after slippage on the fill. If the floor is checked against the gross projection it lets through trades that are thinner than they look. So the rule is: estimated move, minus round-trip fee, minus expected slippage, must exceed the floor. That is the honest waterline, and it is higher than the raw fee.
