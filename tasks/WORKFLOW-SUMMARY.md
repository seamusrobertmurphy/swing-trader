# Day-Trader Workflow: Plain-Language Walkthrough

_Written 2026-06-24 as a spoken-style overview of the whole system across the three main notebooks. Meant to be read aloud or listened to. Numbers are drawn from the build log in CLAUDE.md and the notebooks themselves._

## The big picture, in one breath

This project is a research system for swing and day trading spot crypto, mostly on Binance, with US equities through Alpaca planned for later. It is deliberately built so that no real money moves until a single switch is armed. Everything you are about to hear is the machinery for answering one question honestly: is there a real, repeatable edge here, big enough to survive trading fees, or are we just looking at noise that flatters us in a backtest. The honest answer today is that we have not found a tradable edge yet, but we have found exactly where to keep digging, and we have built the discipline to tell the difference between a real signal and a lucky one.

The work lives in three notebooks, and they form a pipeline. Notebook one, Trader Metrics, is the scan: it looks at the live market and ranks what looks like a buy right now. Notebook two, Trader Controls, is the set of fences: it decides whether a candidate is even allowed to trade, and how big the position should be. Notebook three, Trader Execution, is the laboratory: it builds the historical dataset, engineers the features, trains the model that is supposed to power the entries, and tests it the hard way, out of sample and after fees. Think of it as scout, then risk officer, then scientist.

## Notebook one, Trader Metrics: the live scan

This notebook touches no money and needs no secret keys. It reaches out to the exchange through a library called ccxt, pulls the most recent daily candles for the most heavily traded coins paired with USDT, and computes a stack of classic indicators on each one. Among them: a Kalman filter, which is a way of smoothing the price to see the underlying trend through the noise; Bollinger Bands, which widen when the market is volatile and pinch in when it is quiet; the Ichimoku cloud, which maps trend over a nine to twenty-six day horizon; AMAT and RSI, which read momentum and whether a coin is overbought or oversold; and the Choppiness Index, which tells you whether price is trending cleanly or just chopping sideways.

On top of those it runs what we call the Four Vote score. Four overlapping signals each cast a vote, plus one for a buy and minus one for a sell. The first vote is MACD, which tracks momentum and only acts on a guarded crossover, meaning a crossover that has passed an extra safety check so we are not whipsawed by every wiggle. The second is a moving-average crossover, fast average above slow is a buy, below is a sell. The third is a Fibonacci rule around the so-called golden pocket, the zone where pullbacks often pause. The fourth is a candlestick rule that looks for an engulfing bar, where one candle completely swallows the previous one, often a reversal sign. The votes are summed into a weighted score, and a trade only fires when the score crosses a threshold, normally a net of two. This is also the notebook where the triple-Supertrend trend vote from the live bot gets introduced, three volatility-based trailing bands that must all agree on an uptrend, gated by the two-hundred-period moving average. That trend agreement later becomes a first-class input to the model in notebook three.

The most important thing in notebook one is the sanity-check backtest near the end, because it sets the honest tone for the whole project. We ran the engine over ten coins on hourly data across about forty days, a window that happened to be a broad crypto downtrend, charging a tenth of a percent per side in fees. The result: the engine lost money on every single coin. But it lost roughly half of what simply holding the coins would have lost. Read that carefully. Losing less than buy-and-hold during a falling market is not skill. It is just the result of sitting in cash through part of the decline. That is a drawdown-reduction property, not an edge, and it does not survive into a rising or sideways market where being absent means missing the gains. The win rates, somewhere between twenty and fifty percent, confirm there is no demonstrated predictive skill yet. This is the moment the project committed to a rule it has kept ever since: only an out-of-sample, multi-regime, after-fee result that clearly beats both buy-and-hold and a coin flip is worth real money.

## Notebook two, Trader Controls: the fences

If notebook one is the scout, notebook two is the risk officer who decides what is allowed. Its whole philosophy is captured in one line from the notebook: this is a selective, high-conviction swing strategy, not a scalper. A trade held for a clean three to five percent move does not care about a fifth of a percent round-trip fee. A trade chasing half a percent gets eaten by that fee. So the system enforces a floor, and refuses to play games at the margin.

There are three stacked defences against the most common way a trading model fools itself, which is taking lots of tiny trades that look busy and profitable on paper while fees quietly drain the account. Defence one is a hard cap on trades per day, written in code, so the model physically cannot churn. Defence two is the minimum-edge floor: a trade is refused unless its expected move, after fees and after slippage, clears a floor that sits well above the cost of trading, somewhere in the region of one and a half to two percent. Crucially, that floor is set by the operator and reviewed weekly. It is held outside the model's reach, because a model allowed to lower its own bar would lower it to book more wins. Defence three is out-of-sample validation only: tune on a training stretch, score once on an untouched test stretch, move forward, repeat, and report only the after-fee, multi-regime aggregate.

Before sizing, every coin must pass a four-gate screen. Gate one is liquidity, measured by twenty-four-hour quote volume, to throw out coins too thin to trade. Gate two is the ATR band, the average true range as a percent of price, which must sit inside a window: above a floor so the coin actually moves enough to reach the take-profit, and below a ceiling so it is not so violent that it gaps straight through the stop. Gate three is the spread, the gap between the best buy and sell price, rejected if it is wide enough to eat the edge. Gate four is history length, to throw out coins too young to have a meaningful indicator history. The survivors, normally fifteen to twenty-five coins, are the universe the model is allowed to act on, and we would typically hold three or four positions at once.

Position sizing keeps the dollar risk roughly constant across coins. A calmer coin earns a larger position, a livelier one a smaller position, so that the loss if the stop is hit is about the same in dollars either way. This is the standard volatility-scaled, ATR-based approach, and it pushes us toward a few larger positions rather than many tiny clips that waste edge on fees. The notebook also fixes the trading style firmly in the swing band, days to about two weeks, and rules out scalping and day-trading because of fees and the pattern-day-trader rule, and rules out long-term position-trading because it underuses the signal. The hold period itself becomes something the out-of-sample search picks rather than a number we guess. Finally, notebook two lays down the frozen harness idea: the yardstick, the fee assumptions, the test windows, the regime set, are all frozen and operator-owned, and every walk-forward run appends one line to a log that the operator reviews weekly. The model never gets to move the goalposts.

## Notebook three, Trader Execution: the laboratory

This is the heaviest notebook and the heart of the research. It is where the dataset is built, the features are engineered, the model is trained, and the verdict is reached.

### Where the data comes from

The live scan in notebook one is the right tool for a decision at the open, but it is not what we train or test on. For training we need history that is clean, reproducible, and free of a subtle trap called survivorship bias, which is the mistake of only studying the coins that are still alive today and quietly ignoring the ones that died. The data comes from the official Binance public archive at data.binance.vision, which serves monthly per-coin files of open, high, low, close, and volume bars, one file per time interval, straight from the exchange and checksummed so the build is deterministic. A crawler called acquire_vision walks the full historical list of coins, which turns out to be around six hundred and twelve USDT pairs, of which roughly a third are delisted, dead coins. Including those dead coins is what makes the dataset survivorship-complete, and it matters enormously, because a strategy that looks brilliant on the survivors can fall apart once you include the coins that went to zero.

We build the data at more than one time resolution, because the time frame turns out to be the whole game. There is a one-hour frame and a four-hour frame, with a daily frame planned. The four-hour dataset is the current working one: about five hundred and sixty-seven coins and ninety-five features. The one-hour dataset on disk right now is an older, smaller forty-seven-coin build. Everything is stored in a columnar format called Parquet, which preserves the exact data types so that timestamps do not silently corrupt when the data is reloaded, a bug that genuinely broke the train-test split earlier and is the reason Parquet is now mandatory.

Every coin must pass a data-quality gate before it is allowed into training: at most two percent of its expected hourly bars can be missing, and no single gap can exceed seventy-two hours, because the features assume regularly spaced bars. And the same four-gate screen from notebook two is replayed bar by bar across history, so that a given coin-hour row is only kept if that coin would have passed the screen as of that exact moment. Because the historical archive does not carry the bid-ask spread, the spread gate in history is approximated by a high-low estimator called Corwin-Schultz, while the live screen still uses the real spread.

### What the features are

A feature is just a number computed from the price history that the model can learn from, and every feature in this system obeys two rules: it is causal, meaning it only ever uses information available at that moment and never peeks at the future, and it is scale-invariant, meaning it works the same whether a coin trades at three cents or sixty thousand dollars. There are several families. The wall-clock family and the intraday family are the same indicator ideas computed over two sets of time windows, one tied to daily rhythms and one shorter. There is an in-house technical-analysis block with the familiar oscillators: Williams %R, Stochastic, CCI, money-flow and accumulation measures, the ADX trend-strength family, and Aroon. There are optional blocks from two outside libraries, pandas-ta and TA-Lib, that add things those libraries uniquely contribute, like Parabolic SAR, Hilbert-transform cycle features, and a family of candlestick patterns. There is a trade-flow block that measures the imbalance between aggressive buyers and sellers. And then there are the newer, more interesting families: the triple-Supertrend block, which encodes how many of the three trend bands agree and how far price sits from them; a BTC block, which is the first family not derived from the coin's own price, measuring how the coin moves relative to Bitcoin, its lead-lag and relative strength; a multi-timeframe block that pulls four-hour, daily, and weekly context onto each bar without looking ahead; and a Modern Adaptive Supertrend with an efficiency-ratio gate that filters out roughly eighty percent of the false trend flips. All told, somewhere around eighty-four features on the one-hour rebuild and ninety-five on the four-hour.

### What we are trying to predict, and how we grade it

The thing the model predicts is the label, and the label is an ATR-scaled triple barrier. From each candidate entry bar we draw two lines off the coin's own volatility: an upper barrier at two ATR above, the take-profit, and a lower barrier at one ATR below, the stop. The trade is labelled a win, a one, if price reaches the upper barrier before the lower one within a forty-eight-bar horizon, and a zero otherwise. This target, stop, and horizon are not sacred; they are swept and tuned. To grade fairly, we hold out the final year of history completely out of sample, train on everything before it, leave an embargo gap equal to the label horizon so the training and test data cannot bleed into each other, and we score the test set exactly once. Nothing is kept unless it beats both buy-and-hold and a coin flip on that out-of-sample, after-fee scoreboard.

### The visualizations, and why they exist

A large part of notebook three is now devoted to seeing the entries and exits on real candlesticks, because numbers alone hide what the model is actually doing. There is a gallery that shows several coins as full candlestick charts with volume, overlaid with the trend geometry: the three Supertrend bands and the two-hundred-period moving average. On each chart the entries are marked where all three bands agree on an uptrend and price holds above the moving average, and the exits are marked and coloured by what closed the trade: a stop, a take-profit, or a time stop. Beneath every chart there is a written guide explaining how to read it.

There is also a set of historical views, because a single recent window hides how the rule behaves across different market conditions. One view walks the same rule across years and regimes, the 2019 rally, the COVID crash and recovery, the 2020 bull start, the 2021 blow-off top and May crash, the ETF run-up, the post-halving chop, with a note that through the 2022 bear leg the rule correctly fires zero entries and stands aside. Another view shows the same rule at different timeline lengths, demonstrating that switching to a coarser frame buys you more history at the same legibility. And a final view zooms all the way into the biggest individual winners and losers and annotates each with the drivers that fired it: how many bands agreed, which side of the moving average price was on, the RSI and the volatility. The winners show the trailing stop letting a trend run; the losers show the same clean-looking entry reversing within a few bars. That picture of entries firing near tops and getting stopped a few bars later is the single most important thing to see, because it is the visual form of the central finding.

### The rest of the pipeline

After the visualizations, notebook three does variable selection using an elastic-net method on the training data only, to see which features carry weight. It trains a model, and not just one: the assessment stage runs a zoo of models, three gradient-boosting methods, logistic regression, a random forest, and a stacked ensemble, and grades them with a caret-style table that flags overfitting by comparing in-sample to cross-validated error. Then comes the stability stage, which is the toughest test, a Monte Carlo robustness check that bootstraps and reshuffles the trade sequence thousands of times to ask whether the result could easily have come from luck.

## What we are finding, in plain terms

The headline finding is blunt and it is honest: at the one-hour frame, predicting the direction of the next move is sitting right at the efficient-market floor. In plain language, the market at that time scale is close to a coin flip, and once you subtract trading costs you are underwater. The model zoo lands at an area-under-the-curve of about one half, which is the number you would get from random guessing. The Monte Carlo test on the Supertrend baseline showed about a ninety-nine point seven percent probability of losing money across ten thousand simulated runs, which is worse than a coin flip. This was independently echoed by the author of the very Supertrend method we use, who found it sits around forty-eight percent, bleeds out on the one-hour and below, and only holds up at the four-hour and above.

The base rate of the label tells the same story arithmetically. With a two-ATR target against a one-ATR stop, you need to win about a third of the time just to break even on the geometry, before fees. The actual base rate is about thirty-one percent, which is below that breakeven of a third. So the default label has slightly negative expectancy even before you pay a single fee. Every attempt to fix this by sweeping the label geometry or stretching the horizon came back as a no-go, with win rates a couple of percentage points below breakeven.

Moving to the four-hour frame helps, but not enough. The model's ranking ability rises from about one half to about one half plus five hundredths, call it fifty-five percent, which is a real improvement and confirms that coarser frames carry more signal. Adding the daily and weekly context nudges it a little further and reduces the bleed. But every single setup is still a no-go after fees, because the model only ranks slightly better than a coin flip and the two-ATR-against-one-ATR barrier, combined with the cost of the round trip, sinks it.

We also spent real effort trying to sharpen the entry, and journaled it honestly as a dead end. Range and volatility entry conditions never paid. Trend and momentum conditions paid only marginally. One early, BTC-trend-gated entry that fired about five bars ahead of the Supertrend flip lifted a single out-of-sample year to breakeven, but when we walk-forward tested it across many half-year windows it was positive in only about a third of them and had a negative average edge, which means it was a regime artifact, not a durable edge. The conclusion there is firm: more price-derived entry features will not cross the fee line at this frame.

The one genuinely encouraging finding, and the reason the project is not stuck, is cross-sectional relative strength. Instead of asking "will this coin go up", which is the hard, near-impossible direction question, cross-sectional ranking asks "which coins are strongest right now relative to the others", and bets on the strong against the weak. When we rank the available coins each bar into thirds and compare the top third against the bottom third, the relative-strength signal is real and broad: twenty-five of forty-two momentum and trend signals show a positive, train-and-test sign-stable gap between the top and bottom thirds, and thirty-three of forty-two have the top third beating the market. The most robust of these is the Modern Adaptive Supertrend direction. This is categorically different from the entry-timing work, which had no stable signal at all.

## The margins, the actual money math

Here is where the encouraging finding meets the hard wall. The cost of a round trip on Binance is about a fifth of a percent, a little less if you pay fees in BNB. The whole universe, traded on this label, has an after-fee baseline of about minus zero point three eight percent per trade. That is the average trade, and it is negative. The daily and weekly context improves that bleed from about minus zero point three eight to about minus zero point three one percent per trade, better, but still negative. And the best top third we can isolate by relative strength, using the daily Supertrend signal, comes in at about minus zero point one four percent per trade. Read those three numbers together. The best basket we can build beats the market, it is the least-negative of the lot, but it is still below zero. The edge is real and it is broad, but it is currently drowned by the negative baseline of trading this label at this frame after fees. It is not absent, which is the whole point. It is underwater by a controllable margin.

## Why it is still a no-go, and where it is weak

It is a no-go for one reason stated three ways. The model cannot predict short-horizon direction better than a coin flip at the one-hour frame, the default label has slightly negative expectancy before fees even are paid, and the genuine cross-sectional edge we did find, while real, still lands a hair below the waterline after costs. The weakness is concentrated in two places: the decision frame is too fast, where the market is most efficient and fees bite hardest per unit of move, and the label demands too many round trips for the edge per trip to clear the fee. There is also a structural constraint we cannot trade our way around: the brief forbids shorting, so even a perfect "which coins are weakest" signal cannot be sold, only avoided. That caps how much of the cross-sectional spread we can actually capture.

## What we need to improve, the levers

The fix is not to tune the model harder. We have established that direction prediction at this frame is at the efficiency floor, and tuning a coin-flip yields a tuned coin-flip. The levers that actually move the result are about changing the problem. First, go coarser and slower: a four-hour or daily decision frame, with a longer-horizon label that needs fewer round trips, so the edge per trade has room to clear the fee. Second, gate by regime: only deploy when the broad market, proxied by Bitcoin, is trending up, since the cross-sectional long-only edge is strongest in an up market and the rule already stands aside cleanly in bear legs. Third, and most promising, lean into cross-sectional ranking rather than absolute timing, because that is the one place a real, broad, train-and-test-stable signal showed up. The next build is exactly this: a regime-gated, cross-sectional, coarser-frame strategy, and the task file for it is the live working document.

## What is documented where, and the honest gaps

Mapping it to the three notebooks so you know where everything lives. Notebook one holds the live scan, the indicator stack, the Four Vote score, and the sanity-check backtest that set the honest tone. Notebook two holds the four-gate screen, the edge sizing, the fee fences, the stacked defences, the hold-period reasoning, and the frozen-harness discipline. Notebook three holds the survivorship-complete data pipeline, the feature families, the candlestick and trend visualizations including the new historical and critical-trade views, the variable selection, the model training and the model zoo, the tuning, and the Monte Carlo stability test.

Now the gaps, stated plainly because the standing rule is that if it is not in the three notebooks it is not part of the workflow. The newest and most important findings are not yet written into notebook three as their own sections. Specifically: the four-hour versus one-hour frame comparison, the entry-sharpening dead-end investigation, and above all the cross-sectional relative-strength result with its margin numbers, all currently live in standalone scripts and task files rather than in the notebook narrative. The exit-geometry and label sweeps are referenced but their full scoreboards are not all rendered inline. So by the project's own standard, the cross-sectional opening, which is the single most important current finding, is not yet truly part of the workflow. The clear next step is to integrate those three things into notebook three as first-class, documented sections, the same way the visualizations were just integrated, so that the notebook tells the whole story end to end and nothing important lives only in a script. That is the work I would do next.
